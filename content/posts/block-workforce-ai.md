Block laid off 4,000 people — roughly 40% of its workforce — and the market rewarded it with a 24% stock surge. Read that again. Four thousand people lost their jobs, and Wall Street celebrated.

This isn't an anomaly. It's a pattern. And it tells us something important about where work is heading.

## The Numbers

Let's start with what actually happened. Block — the company behind Square and Cash App — cut approximately 4,000 employees, reducing its workforce by about 40%. Jack Dorsey framed it not as a cost-cutting measure but as a strategic realignment. In his words, the company is betting on "intelligence tools" and smaller, more focused teams.

The market's response was immediate and unambiguous: Block's stock jumped 24%.

This is the part that should make you pay attention. Not because it's surprising — we've seen this pattern before — but because of what it signals about how the market values human labor versus technological capability.

## Block Is Not Alone

Block's move fits into a broader pattern that's been accelerating throughout 2025 and into 2026.

**Chegg**, the education technology company, cut 45% of its workforce, explicitly citing AI as the reason. Their core business — homework help and tutoring — was being eaten alive by ChatGPT and similar tools. Students who once paid $15 a month for Chegg's services discovered they could get better answers for free. The layoffs weren't a choice. They were an acknowledgment that the old model was dead.

**Pinterest** cut 15% of its workforce as part of a restructuring toward AI-driven content curation and advertising. The people who manually curated and categorized content? Replaced by algorithms that do it faster, cheaper, and at a scale no human team could match.

**CrowdStrike** laid off 500 employees — about 5% of its workforce — while simultaneously investing heavily in AI-driven security tools. The company's thesis: AI can detect and respond to threats faster than human analysts. The remaining workforce would be smaller but more effective.

Each of these companies told a slightly different story. But the underlying logic was the same: we can do more with fewer people, and the tools exist now to prove it.

## The Dorsey Thesis

Dorsey's framing is worth examining because it's the most explicit version of an argument many executives are making privately.

His position isn't that people are bad or unnecessary. It's that the ratio of people to output has fundamentally changed. A team of 10 with the right AI tools can now produce what used to require a team of 50. Not in theory — in practice, right now.

This isn't about replacing people with chatbots. It's about rearchitecting how work gets done.

Consider what's happening in software development. Tools like Claude Code, GitHub Copilot, and OpenClaw are enabling individual developers to ship features that previously required entire teams. A solo developer with the right AI toolkit can build, test, deploy, and monitor applications at a pace that would have been unthinkable two years ago.

I've seen this firsthand. My own setup — four AI agents with 20+ specialized skills, running on a single VPS — handles content creation, code reviews, deployment pipelines, and monitoring. The work that gets done overnight, while I sleep, would have required a small team working in shifts.

## The Market's Message

When a company lays off 40% of its people and the stock goes up 24%, the market is sending a clear message: it believes the remaining 60% — augmented by AI tools — will be more productive and more profitable than the original 100%.

This is a fundamentally different kind of layoff than what we saw in previous economic downturns. During the 2008 financial crisis, companies cut people because revenue was falling and they needed to survive. The layoffs were defensive.

What's happening now is offensive. Companies are cutting not because they're struggling, but because they believe they've found a better way to operate. They're not shrinking — they're restructuring around a new model of work.

And the market is rewarding them for it.

## What This Actually Means

Let's be clear about what this doesn't mean: it doesn't mean that half of all jobs are about to disappear overnight. That's the lazy take, and it's wrong.

What it means is that the *unit economics of knowledge work* are changing. The cost of producing a unit of software, content, analysis, or design is dropping rapidly. Companies that figure out how to operate at the new cost structure will outcompete those that don't.

This creates two distinct pressures:

**For companies:** The pressure to adopt AI tools and restructure teams is becoming existential. If your competitor can ship the same product with 40% fewer people, your margins look increasingly untenable. You either adapt or you get outcompeted.

**For workers:** The value proposition shifts from "I can do this task" to "I can orchestrate AI tools to do this task 10x faster and better." The people who thrive in this environment won't be the ones who resist the tools — they'll be the ones who master them.

## The Uncomfortable Middle

Here's the part nobody wants to talk about: the transition is going to be messy.

Not everyone can retool overnight. Not every job that gets eliminated will be replaced by a higher-value AI-augmented role. Some people will fall through the cracks. Some companies will cut too deep and lose institutional knowledge they can't replace. Some will discover that the AI tools they bet on don't actually work as well as the demo suggested.

The 4,000 people who lost their jobs at Block aren't abstractions. They're engineers, designers, project managers, and support staff who had mortgages, families, and plans. The stock going up doesn't make their situation less real.

But acknowledging the human cost doesn't change the trajectory. The economics are too compelling. When you can genuinely do more with less — not just on a spreadsheet, but in actual output — the pressure to restructure is irresistible.

## Where This Goes

I don't think we're heading toward a world with dramatically fewer jobs. History suggests that technological revolutions create new categories of work even as they eliminate old ones. The printing press didn't end employment — it created publishers, journalists, and librarians. The internet didn't kill commerce — it created an entirely new economy.

What I do think is happening: the nature of valuable work is shifting faster than at any point in modern history. The window for adaptation is shorter. The gap between "AI-augmented worker" and "traditional worker" in terms of output is widening.

Block's layoffs and the market's response are a data point. Chegg, Pinterest, and CrowdStrike are additional data points. Together, they paint a picture of an economy that is rapidly repricing human labor against AI capability.

The question isn't whether this trend will continue. It will.

The question is whether you're positioning yourself on the right side of it.

Not by fighting the tools. By mastering them.
